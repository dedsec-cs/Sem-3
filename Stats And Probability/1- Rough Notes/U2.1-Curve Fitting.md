## Curve Fitting

Curve fitting is a fundamental technique in statistics and data analysis used to **construct a mathematical function** that best represents the relationship between a set of observed data points. The goal is to find a curve that passes as close as possible to all the given data points.

---

### WTF Is Curve Fitting In First Place

Imagine you have collected some **data points**, maybe the price of a stock over several days, or the temperature of a cooling object over time. If you plot these points on a graph, they might show a certain **pattern** or **trend**.

**Curve fitting** is the process of finding a **formula** (an equation, like $y = 2x + 5$ or $y = 3x^2$) whose graph (the "curve") closely matches that pattern in your data. It's like drawing the best smooth line or curve through a scatter of dots.

* **Input:** A set of **paired data points** $\left(x_1, y_1\right), \left(x_2, y_2\right), \ldots, \left(x_n, y_n\right)$.
* **Process:** Choosing a specific type of function (e.g., a straight line, a parabola, an exponential curve) and finding the specific **parameters** (like the slope and intercept of a line) that make the function's graph fit the data best.
* **Output:** The **equation** of the "best-fit" curve.


---

### Why U Fit Curves

There are two main reasons for fitting curves to data:

1.  **Estimation and Prediction:** Once you have the equation of the curve, you can use it to **estimate** the value of $y$ for an **intermediate** value of $x$ (interpolation) or **predict** the value of $y$ for a value of $x$ **outside** the range of your original data (extrapolation). For example, if your curve fits the relationship between years of schooling ($x$) and income ($y$), you can predict the income of someone with a certain number of years of schooling.
2.  **Summarizing and Understanding the Relationship:** The equation itself provides a concise mathematical **model** of the relationship between the variables. The parameters of the fitted curve (like the slope) give insight into the nature and strength of that relationship.

---

### How They're Fitted

Curves are fitted by defining a measure of how "good" the fit is and then trying to **minimize** the badness of the fit.

* **The Problem:** When you draw a curve through data points, most of the points will **not** lie exactly on the curve. The vertical distance between an actual data point ($y_{actual}$) and the corresponding point on the fitted curve ($\hat{y}_{predicted}$) is called the **residual** or **error** ($e$). $e = y_{actual} - \hat{y}_{predicted}$.
* **The Goal:** We want to find the curve whose equation makes the total size of all these errors (residuals) as **small** as possible.
* **The Common Technique:** The most widely used technique is the **Method of Least Squares**.

---
---

## Method Of Least Squares

The **Method of Least Squares** is the standard procedure for finding the curve that best fits a set of data.

* **The Idea:** Simply adding up the errors is not useful because positive errors (points above the curve) and negative errors (points below the curve) would cancel each other out.
* **The Solution:** To overcome the cancellation problem, we **square** each error (residual) before summing them up. Squaring the errors ensures that:
    1.  All contributions to the total "badness of fit" are **positive**.
    2.  Larger errors are **magnified** (a residual of 10 squared is 100, a residual of 1 squared is 1). This heavily penalizes curves that miss a few points by a large distance.

* **The Formula:** The quantity we seek to minimize is the **Sum of Squared Errors (SSE)** or $S$:
    $$S = \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$$
    where $y_i$ is the actual $y$-value and $\hat{y}_i$ is the $y$-value predicted by the fitted curve.

* **The Technique:** We treat the SSE ($S$) as a function of the unknown parameters (e.g., $a$ and $b$ for a straight line $y = a + bx$). To find the values of the parameters that **minimize** $S$, we use calculus:
    1.  We take the **partial derivative** of $S$ with respect to each unknown parameter.
    2.  We set each of these derivatives equal to **zero**.
    3.  This results in a system of equations called the **Normal Equations**.
    4.  We solve the Normal Equations simultaneously to find the optimal values for the parameters.

# Fitting A Straight Line

The simplest and most common type of curve to fit is a **straight line**, also known as **Linear Regression**. The equation for a straight line is:

$$\hat{y} = a + bx$$

Where:
* $\hat{y}$ is the **predicted** value of the dependent variable.
* $x$ is the value of the independent variable.
* $a$ is the **y-intercept** (the predicted value of $\hat{y}$ when $x=0$).
* $b$ is the **slope** (how much $\hat{y}$ changes for a one-unit change in $x$).

Our goal is to find the values of $a$ and $b$ that minimize the Sum of Squared Errors, $S = \sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 = \sum (y_i - (a + bx_i))^2$.

---

### Normal Equations For A Straight Line

To find the values of $a$ and $b$ that minimize $S$, we take the partial derivatives of $S$ with respect to $a$ and $b$ and set them to zero. This process yields the following two simultaneous equations, called the **Normal Equations for a Straight Line**:

1.  $$\sum y = na + b \sum x$$
2.  $$\sum xy = a \sum x + b \sum x^2$$

Where:
* $n$ is the number of data points.
* $\sum y$ is the sum of all the $y$-values.
* $\sum x$ is the sum of all the $x$-values.
* $\sum xy$ is the sum of the products of each $x_i$ and $y_i$.
* $\sum x^2$ is the sum of the squares of each $x_i$.

By calculating the four sums ($\sum x$, $\sum y$, $\sum x^2$, $\sum xy$) from the raw data and substituting them into these two equations, we can solve for the two unknowns, $a$ and $b$.

---

### Trick To Remember

The Normal Equations can be conceptually remembered by starting with the original equation of the line, $\hat{y} = a + bx$, and performing two operations:

1.  **First Equation:** Sum the entire equation across all $n$ data points.
    $$\sum \hat{y} = \sum (a + bx)$$
    Since the sum of the predicted $\hat{y}$ values is approximately the same as the sum of the actual $y$ values ($\sum \hat{y} \approx \sum y$), and since $a$ is a constant summed $n$ times, we get:
    $$\sum y = na + b \sum x$$

2.  **Second Equation:** Multiply the original equation by $x$ and then sum the resulting terms across all $n$ data points.
    $$x\hat{y} = x(a + bx) \implies x\hat{y} = ax + bx^2$$
    Summing this (and replacing $\sum x\hat{y}$ with $\sum xy$):
    $$\sum xy = a \sum x + b \sum x^2$$

---

### How To Create Normal Equation Of Any Straight Line

The general method for deriving the normal equations for **any** model (not just a straight line) is based on the parameters of the model itself.

For the model $\hat{y} = a + b \cdot f(x)$, where $f(x)$ is a function of $x$:

1.  **Identify the parameters** to be estimated. For $\hat{y} = a + bx$, the parameters are $a$ and $b$.
2.  **Identify the "coefficients"** of the parameters in the equation.
    * The coefficient of $a$ is **$1$**.
    * The coefficient of $b$ is **$x$**.
3.  **The Rule:** The Normal Equations are created by:
    * **Equation 1 (for parameter $a$):** Multiply the original equation by the coefficient of $a$ (which is $1$) and then sum.
        $$\sum (\hat{y} \cdot 1) = \sum ((a + bx) \cdot 1) \implies \sum y = na + b \sum x$$
    * **Equation 2 (for parameter $b$):** Multiply the original equation by the coefficient of $b$ (which is $x$) and then sum.
        $$\sum (\hat{y} \cdot x) = \sum ((a + bx) \cdot x) \implies \sum xy = a \sum x + b \sum x^2$$

This rule is a powerful shortcut for writing the Normal Equations for other types of curves as well.

---

### Solve Multiple Different Cases Of Normal Equation Writing for Straight lines

Let's apply the rule to slight variations of the straight-line model.

#### Case 1: Standard Straight Line

* **Model:** $\hat{y} = a + bx$
* **Parameters:** $a$ (coefficient $1$), $b$ (coefficient $x$).
* **Normal Equations:**
    1.  Multiply by $1$: $\sum y = n a + b \sum x$
    2.  Multiply by $x$: $\sum xy = a \sum x + b \sum x^2$

#### Case 2: Line Through the Origin

* **Model:** $\hat{y} = bx$ (The $y$-intercept $a$ is forced to be $0$).
* **Parameter:** $b$ (coefficient $x$).
* **Normal Equation (Only one equation is needed to solve for one unknown $b$):**
    1.  Multiply by the coefficient of $b$, which is $x$:
        $$\sum (\hat{y} \cdot x) = \sum (bx \cdot x)$$
        $$\sum xy = b \sum x^2$$

#### Case 3: A Simple Shifted Curve (Example for the power of the method)

* **Model:** $\hat{y} = a + b(\log x)$ (This is a straight line if plotted against $\log x$).
* **Parameters:** $a$ (coefficient $1$), $b$ (coefficient $\log x$).
* **Normal Equations:**
    1.  Multiply by $1$:
        $$\sum y = n a + b \sum \log x$$
    2.  Multiply by $\log x$:
        $$\sum (y \log x) = a \sum \log x + b \sum (\log x)^2$$
    *This shows the method works for any linear combination of parameters, where you just substitute the coefficient term.*

---

That's a great way to practice setting up the equations! The key, as discussed, is identifying the parameters and their coefficients.

## 1. Linearizable Models (Linear in Parameters)

These models are linear if we define new variables based on the original $x$ and $y$. We will treat the coefficients of the parameters as the terms used to create the Normal Equations.

### Example 1.1: Inverse and Power Fit

* **Model:** $\hat{y} = A + B \cdot \frac{1}{x^2}$
    * This is a straight line if plotted against $\frac{1}{x^2}$.
    * **Parameters to solve for:** $A$ and $B$.
    * **Coefficient of $A$:** $1$.
    * **Coefficient of $B$:** $\frac{1}{x^2}$.

#### Normal Equations:

1.  **Multiply by $1$ (coefficient of $A$):**
    $$\sum y = nA + B \sum \frac{1}{x^2}$$

2.  **Multiply by $\frac{1}{x^2}$ (coefficient of $B$):**
    $$\sum \left(y \cdot \frac{1}{x^2}\right) = A \sum \frac{1}{x^2} + B \sum \left(\frac{1}{x^2} \cdot \frac{1}{x^2}\right)$$
    $$\sum \frac{y}{x^2} = A \sum \frac{1}{x^2} + B \sum \frac{1}{x^4}$$

---

### Example 1.2: Exponential Growth/Decay (After Transformation)

* **Original Model (Non-Linear):** $\hat{y} = a e^{bx}$
* **Linearization Step:** Take the natural logarithm ($\ln$) of both sides.
    $$\ln(\hat{y}) = \ln(a) + bx$$
* **Linear Form:** Let $Y = \ln(\hat{y})$, and let $A = \ln(a)$.
    $$Y = A + bx$$
    * **Parameters to solve for:** $A$ and $b$.
    * **Coefficient of $A$:** $1$.
    * **Coefficient of $b$:** $x$.

#### Normal Equations:

1.  **Multiply by $1$ (coefficient of $A$):**
    $$\sum Y = nA + b \sum x$$

2.  **Multiply by $x$ (coefficient of $b$):**
    $$\sum (Y x) = A \sum x + b \sum x^2$$

*(Note: Once $A$ and $b$ are solved, the original parameter $a$ is found by $a = e^A$.)*

---

## 2. Polynomial/Multiple Regression Models (Still Linear in Parameters)

These models are also formally **linear in the parameters**, $a, b, c$, etc., and require the standard least squares technique, resulting in as many Normal Equations as there are parameters.

### Example 2.1: Quadratic (Parabolic) Fit

* **Model:** $\hat{y} = a + bx + cx^2$
    * **Parameters to solve for:** $a$, $b$, and $c$.
    * **Coefficient of $a$:** $1$.
    * **Coefficient of $b$:** $x$.
    * **Coefficient of $c$:** $x^2$.

#### Normal Equations (3 equations for 3 unknowns):

1.  **Multiply by $1$ (coefficient of $a$):**
    $$\sum y = n a + b \sum x + c \sum x^2$$

2.  **Multiply by $x$ (coefficient of $b$):**
    $$\sum (y \cdot x) = a \sum x + b \sum (x \cdot x) + c \sum (x^2 \cdot x)$$
    $$\sum xy = a \sum x + b \sum x^2 + c \sum x^3$$

3.  **Multiply by $x^2$ (coefficient of $c$):**
    $$\sum (y \cdot x^2) = a \sum x^2 + b \sum (x \cdot x^2) + c \sum (x^2 \cdot x^2)$$
    $$\sum x^2 y = a \sum x^2 + b \sum x^3 + c \sum x^4$$

---

### Example 2.2: Line with a Logarithmic Term

* **Model:** $\hat{y} = C_1 + C_2 \log(x)$
    * This is a straight line if plotted against $\log(x)$.
    * **Parameters to solve for:** $C_1$ and $C_2$.
    * **Coefficient of $C_1$:** $1$.
    * **Coefficient of $C_2$:** $\log(x)$.

#### Normal Equations:

1.  **Multiply by $1$ (coefficient of $C_1$):**
    $$\sum y = n C_1 + C_2 \sum \log(x)$$

2.  **Multiply by $\log(x)$ (coefficient of $C_2$):**
    $$\sum (y \cdot \log x) = C_1 \sum \log x + C_2 \sum (\log x \cdot \log x)$$
    $$\sum y \log x = C_1 \sum \log x + C_2 \sum (\log x)^2$$

# Fitting Of Exponential Curves

## 📈 Fitting of Exponential Curves

An exponential curve describes a relationship where a change in the independent variable ($x$) leads to a proportional change in the dependent variable ($y$). The most common forms are $\hat{y} = ab^x$ and $\hat{y} = a e^{bx}$.

### A. General Equation Creation (Model 1: $\hat{y} = a b^x$)

The goal is to transform the non-linear model $\hat{y} = a b^x$ into a straight-line form, $Y = A + Bx$, so we can use the Normal Equations.

#### 1. Linearization Step

Take the logarithm (usually the natural log, $\ln$, or log base 10, $\log$) of both sides of the original equation:

$$\hat{y} = a b^x$$

Using the **Product Rule** and the **Power Rule** of logarithms:

$$\log(\hat{y}) = \log(a b^x)$$
$$\log(\hat{y}) = \log(a) + \log(b^x)$$
$$\mathbf{\log(\hat{y}) = \log(a) + x \log(b)}$$

#### 2. Defining New Variables

We define new variables to match the straight-line equation $Y = A + Bx$:

* **New Dependent Variable ($Y$):** $Y = \log(\hat{y})$
* **New Y-Intercept ($A$):** $A = \log(a)$
* **New Slope ($B$):** $B = \log(b)$
* **New Independent Variable ($X$):** $X = x$

The linear form is: $\mathbf{Y = A + B X}$

#### 3. Normal Equations for the Linearized Form

Since the new equation $Y = A + BX$ is a straight line, we use the standard Normal Equations (with $Y$ replacing $y$ and $X$ replacing $x$):

1.  $$\sum Y = n A + B \sum X$$
2.  $$\sum XY = A \sum X + B \sum X^2$$

#### 4. Finding the Original Parameters ($a$ and $b$)

After solving the Normal Equations to find the values for $A$ and $B$, you must revert them back to the original parameters, $a$ and $b$, using the inverse operation of the logarithm you used.

* If you used **$\ln$**: $\mathbf{a = e^A}$ and $\mathbf{b = e^B}$
* If you used **$\log_{10}$**: $\mathbf{a = 10^A}$ and $\mathbf{b = 10^B}$

---

### B. General Equation Creation (Model 2: $\hat{y} = a e^{bx}$)

This model is common when using natural growth/decay rates.

#### 1. Linearization Step

Take the **Natural Log ($\ln$)** of both sides of the original equation:

$$\hat{y} = a e^{bx}$$

$$\ln(\hat{y}) = \ln(a e^{bx})$$
$$\ln(\hat{y}) = \ln(a) + \ln(e^{bx}) \quad \text{(Product Rule)}$$
$$\mathbf{\ln(\hat{y}) = \ln(a) + bx} \quad \text{(Since } \ln(e^k)=k \text{)}$$

#### 2. Defining New Variables

* **New Dependent Variable ($Y$):** $Y = \ln(\hat{y})$
* **New Y-Intercept ($A$):** $A = \ln(a)$
* **New Slope ($B$):** $B = b$
* **New Independent Variable ($X$):** $X = x$

The linear form is: $\mathbf{Y = A + B X}$

#### 3. Normal Equations

Since the linear form is identical to Model 1, the Normal Equations are the same:

1.  $$\sum Y = n A + B \sum X$$
2.  $$\sum XY = A \sum X + B \sum X^2$$

#### 4. Finding the Original Parameters ($a$ and $b$)

The parameter $B$ is the same as $b$, and $A$ is converted back to $a$:

* $\mathbf{b = B}$
* $\mathbf{a = e^A}$

---

## 📝 Example: Fitting $\hat{y} = a b^x$

Let's find the Normal Equations and the required sums for the data below to fit the curve $\hat{y} = a b^x$. (We will use $\log_{10}$ for this example.)

| $x$ | $y$ |
| :---: | :---: |
| 1 | 3.0 |
| 2 | 4.8 |
| 3 | 7.6 |
| 4 | 12.1 |

### Step 1: Linearization and Identifying Variables

* **Linear Form:** $\log_{10}(\hat{y}) = \log_{10}(a) + x \log_{10}(b)$
* **New Variables:**
    * $Y = \log_{10}(y)$
    * $A = \log_{10}(a)$
    * $B = \log_{10}(b)$
    * $X = x$

### Step 2: Calculate the Required Sums

We need sums of $X$, $Y$, $X^2$, and $XY$. Since $X=x$, we need $\sum x$, $\sum \log_{10}(y)$, $\sum x^2$, and $\sum x \log_{10}(y)$.

| $x$ ($X$) | $y$  | $\log_{10}(y)$ ($Y$)  |   $x^2$ ($X^2$)   | $x \cdot \log_{10}(y)$ ($XY$) |                        |
| :-------: | :--: | :-------------------: | :---------------: | :---------------------------: | ---------------------- |
|     1     | 3.0  |        0.4771         |         1         |            0.4771             |                        |
|     2     | 4.8  |        0.6812         |         4         |            1.3624             |                        |
|     3     | 7.6  |        0.8808         |         9         |            2.6424             |                        |
|     4     | 12.1 |        1.0828         |        16         |            4.3312             |                        |
| **$n=4$** |      | **$\sum Y = 3.1219$** | **$\sum X = 10$** |      **$\sum X^2 = 30$**      | **$\sum XY = 8.8131$** |

### Step 3: Write the Normal Equations

Substitute the sums into the Normal Equations (using $A$ and $B$):

1.  $$\sum Y = n A + B \sum X$$
    $$3.1219 = 4A + 10B \quad \text{(Equation 1)}$$

2.  $$\sum XY = A \sum X + B \sum X^2$$
    $$8.8131 = 10A + 30B \quad \text{(Equation 2)}$$

### Step 4: Solve for $A$ and $B$

1.  Multiply Equation 1 by $2.5$ to match the $A$ coefficient in Equation 2:
    $$2.5 \times (3.1219) = 2.5 \times (4A) + 2.5 \times (10B)$$
    $$7.80475 = 10A + 25B \quad \text{(Equation 3)}$$

2.  Subtract Equation 3 from Equation 2:
    $$(8.8131 - 7.80475) = (10A - 10A) + (30B - 25B)$$
    $$1.00835 = 5B$$
    $$\mathbf{B = 0.20167}$$

3.  Substitute $B$ back into Equation 1:
    $$3.1219 = 4A + 10(0.20167)$$
    $$3.1219 = 4A + 2.0167$$
    $$4A = 3.1219 - 2.0167 = 1.1052$$
    $$\mathbf{A = 0.2763}$$

### Step 5: Find Original Parameters ($a$ and $b$)

Since we used $\log_{10}$:

* $$a = 10^A = 10^{0.2763} \approx \mathbf{1.89}$$
* $$b = 10^B = 10^{0.20167} \approx \mathbf{1.59}$$

### Final Exponential Equation

The best-fit exponential curve is:
$$\mathbf{\hat{y} = 1.89 \cdot (1.59)^x}$$

# 📉 Fitting a Second Order Parabola

A second order parabola is represented by a polynomial of degree two. It's used when the relationship between $x$ and $y$ is **curvilinear** (U-shaped or inverted U-shaped).

### 1. The Model Equation

The general equation for a parabola is:
$$\mathbf{\hat{y} = a + bx + cx^2}$$

* **Parameters to solve for:** $a$ (Y-intercept), $b$ (coefficient of the linear term), and $c$ (coefficient of the quadratic term).
* Since the parameters $a$, $b$, and $c$ all appear linearly (they are not exponents or multiplied by each other), we can use the Method of Least Squares directly, resulting in **three** Normal Equations.

[Image of a scatter plot with a parabolic curve drawn through the data points representing quadratic curve fitting]

---

### 2. General Equation Creation (Normal Equations)

We have three unknown parameters ($a$, $b$, and $c$), so we need three simultaneous Normal Equations. We derive these by multiplying the original model equation by the coefficient of each parameter and then summing the result.

| Parameter | Coefficient in $\hat{y} = a + bx + cx^2$ | Operation (Multiply and Sum) |
| :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **$a$** | $1$ | $\sum (\hat{y} \cdot 1) = \sum (a \cdot 1 + b x \cdot 1 + c x^2 \cdot 1)$ |
| **$b$** | $x$ | $\sum (\hat{y} \cdot x) = \sum (a \cdot x + b x \cdot x + c x^2 \cdot x)$ |
| **$c$** | $x^2$ | $\sum (\hat{y} \cdot x^2) = \sum (a \cdot x^2 + b x \cdot x^2 + c x^2 \cdot x^2)$ |

#### The Three Normal Equations

Setting $\sum \hat{y} \approx \sum y$, the resulting system is:

1.  $$\mathbf{\sum y = n a + b \sum x + c \sum x^2}$$
2.  $$\mathbf{\sum xy = a \sum x + b \sum x^2 + c \sum x^3}$$
3.  $$\mathbf{\sum x^2 y = a \sum x^2 + b \sum x^3 + c \sum x^4}$$

---

### 3. Required Calculations

To solve this system, you must compute the following seven sums from your raw data points $(x_i, y_i)$:

$$n, \quad \sum x, \quad \sum y, \quad \sum x^2, \quad \sum x^3, \quad \sum x^4, \quad \sum xy, \quad \sum x^2y$$

### 4. Solving the System

Once all the required sums are calculated and substituted into the three Normal Equations, you must solve the resulting $3 \times 3$ system of linear equations simultaneously for the unknowns $a$, $b$, and $c$. This can be done using techniques like substitution, elimination, or matrix methods.

---

## 🛠️ Fitting the Power-Law Curve: $PV^r = C$

### 1. Linearization

The goal is to transform the equation into the linear form $\mathbf{Y = A + B X}$.

Starting with the model:
$$P V^r = C$$

Take the natural logarithm ($\ln$) of both sides:
$$\ln(P V^r) = \ln(C)$$

Using the **Product Rule** and **Power Rule** of logarithms:
$$\ln(P) + \ln(V^r) = \ln(C)$$
$$\mathbf{\ln(P) + r \ln(V) = \ln(C)}$$

Now, we rearrange this to match the straight-line form $\mathbf{Y = A + B X}$:

$$\ln(P) = \ln(C) - r \ln(V)$$

### 2. Defining New Variables

* **New Dependent Variable ($Y$):** $Y = \ln(P)$
* **New Independent Variable ($X$):** $X = \ln(V)$
* **New Y-Intercept ($A$):** $A = \ln(C)$
* **New Slope ($B$):** $B = -r$ (Note the negative sign!)

The linear form is: $\mathbf{Y = A + B X}$

### 3. Normal Equations

Since the model is now linear in $A$ and $B$, we use the standard Normal Equations:

1.  $$\mathbf{\sum Y = n A + B \sum X}$$
2.  $$\mathbf{\sum XY = A \sum X + B \sum X^2}$$

### 4. Finding Original Parameters

After solving for $A$ and $B$:
* The exponent $\mathbf{r}$ is found by: $\mathbf{r = -B}$
* The constant $\mathbf{C}$ is found by: $\mathbf{C = e^A}$

## 📝 Example 1: Finding $r$ and $C$ (Basic Case)

**Q1.** Fit the curve $\mathbf{PV^r = C}$ to the following data:

| $P$ | 100 | 50 | 20 |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| $V$ | 1.0 | 2.0 | 4.0 |

#### 1. Calculate the Required Sums

### Fitting an Exponential Curve ($y=ax^b$) (Example)

**Q1.** Fit the following Equation, $y=ax^b$, to the following data:
$$
\text{Model: } \hat{y} = ax^b
$$

| X | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| y | 0.5 | 1.7 | 3.4 | 5.7 | 8.4 |

#### 1. Linearization

Take the natural logarithm ($\ln$) of both sides:
$$\ln(\hat{y}) = \ln(ax^b)$$
$$\ln(\hat{y}) = \ln(a) + b \ln(x)$$

Define the new variables and parameters for the linear form $Y = A + B X$:
$$Y = \ln(y), \quad A = \ln(a), \quad B = b, \quad X = \ln(x)$$

#### 2. Calculate the Required Sums

We need sums of $X$, $Y$, $X^2$, and $XY$. $n=5$.

| $x$ | $y$ | $X = \ln(x)$ | $Y = \ln(y)$ | $X^2 = (\ln x)^2$ | $XY = (\ln x) \cdot (\ln y)$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| 1 | 0.5 | 0.0000 | -0.6931 | 0.0000 | 0.0000 |
| 2 | 1.7 | 0.6931 | 0.5306 | 0.4804 | 0.3677 |
| 3 | 3.4 | 1.0986 | 1.2238 | 1.2070 | 1.3444 |
| 4 | 5.7 | 1.3863 | 1.7405 | 1.9218 | 2.4129 |
| 5 | 8.4 | 1.6094 | 2.1282 | 2.5902 | 3.4241 |
| **Total** | | **$\sum X = 4.7874$** | **$\sum Y = 4.9300$** | **$\sum X^2 = 6.1994$** | **$\sum XY = 7.5491$** |

#### 3. Set Up and Solve Normal Equations

1. $\sum Y = n A + B \sum X \implies \mathbf{4.9300} = 5A + 4.7874B \quad \text{(Eq. 1)}$
2. $\sum XY = A \sum X + B \sum X^2 \implies \mathbf{7.5491} = 4.7874A + 6.1994B \quad \text{(Eq. 2)}$

Solving the system (using substitution/elimination or calculator/software for precision):

From (Eq. 1): $A = \frac{4.9300 - 4.7874B}{5} = 0.9860 - 0.9575B$

Substitute $A$ into (Eq. 2):
$$7.5491 = 4.7874(0.9860 - 0.9575B) + 6.1994B$$
$$7.5491 = 4.7180 - 4.5841B + 6.1994B$$

$$2.8311 = 1.6153B \implies \mathbf{B \approx 1.7527}$$

Substitute $B$ back into the expression for $A$:
$$A = 0.9860 - 0.9575(1.7527) = 0.9860 - 1.6781$$
$$\mathbf{A \approx -0.6921}$$

#### 4. Find Original Parameters ($a$ and $b$)

* $\mathbf{b} = B \approx \mathbf{1.75}$
* $\mathbf{a} = e^A = e^{-0.6921} \approx \mathbf{0.5005}$

#### Final Result

The best-fit exponential curve is:
$$\mathbf{\hat{y} = 0.5005 x^{1.75}}$$
