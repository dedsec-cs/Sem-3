## F-Test (Fisher's F-Distribution)

The **F-test** is a statistical test that uses the **F-distribution** to compare variances. It is named in honor of statistician Sir Ronald A. Fisher.

---

### When to Use the F-Test

The F-test is primarily used in two main contexts:

1.  **Comparing Two Variances:** The most common application of the simple F-test is to determine if the variances (or standard deviations) of **two independent populations** are equal ($\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2$).
    * This is often done as a **preliminary check** before performing a Two-Sample T-Test, which requires the assumption of **equal variances** (homogeneity of variances).
2.  **Comparing Multiple Means (ANOVA):** The F-test is the core test used in **Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)**. In this context, the F-statistic compares the variability *between* group means to the variability *within* groups to determine if at least one group mean is significantly different from the others.

> **Key Difference:** While $Z$-tests and $t$-tests compare **means**, the F-test, in its simplest form, compares **variances**.

---

### F-Test Formula (Comparing Two Variances)

The test statistic for comparing two population variances ($\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$) is the ratio of their corresponding sample variances ($S_1^2$ and $S_2^2$).

$$F_{\text{Calculated}} = \frac{S_1^2}{S_2^2}$$

Where:
* $S_1^2$: Sample Variance of the first group.
* $S_2^2$: Sample Variance of the second group.

#### **Convention for One-Tailed and Two-Tailed Tests**

To simplify the process and use a single F-distribution table (which usually provides critical values for the upper tail), a common convention is to always place the **larger sample variance in the numerator** ($S_{\text{Larger}}^2$) and the **smaller sample variance in the denominator** ($S_{\text{Smaller}}^2$).

$$F_{\text{Calculated}} = \frac{S_{\text{Larger}}^2}{S_{\text{Smaller}}^2}$$

This ensures the calculated $F$-value is always greater than or equal to 1, and the test becomes a **Right-Tailed Test**.

| Type of Test | Ratio Convention | Rejection Region | Critical Value Location |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Two-Tailed** ($H_1: \sigma_1^2 \ne \sigma_2^2$) | Place the **Larger** $S^2$ in the numerator. | The upper tail only (since $F \ge 1$). | Use $\alpha/2$ for the critical value. |
| **Right-Tailed** ($H_1: \sigma_1^2 > \sigma_2^2$) | $S_1^2$ (the variance associated with $\sigma_1^2$) must be in the numerator. | The upper tail only. | Use $\alpha$ for the critical value. |

---

### The F-Distribution and Degrees of Freedom ($df$)

The **F-distribution** is a continuous probability distribution that only takes on **non-negative values** (since variances are non-negative) and is **always skewed to the right** .

#### Degrees of Freedom ($df$)

Unlike the $t$-distribution, the F-distribution requires **two** independent degrees of freedom to define its shape:

1.  **Numerator Degrees of Freedom ($df_1$):** Associated with the variance in the numerator.
    $$df_1 = n_1 - 1$$
2.  **Denominator Degrees of Freedom ($df_2$):** Associated with the variance in the denominator.
    $$df_2 = n_2 - 1$$

When using the convention of putting $S_{\text{Larger}}^2$ in the numerator, the degrees of freedom associated with the larger variance become $df_1$, and the degrees of freedom associated with the smaller variance become $df_2$.

#### Assumptions for the F-Test (Comparing Two Variances)

1.  **Random Samples:** Both samples must be drawn randomly and independently from their respective populations.
2.  **Normality:** Both populations must be **normally distributed**.
    * **Caution:** The F-test is very sensitive to violations of the normality assumption, especially for small sample sizes.

## F-Test Examples (Comparing Two Variances)

### Example 1: Two-Tailed F-Test (Given Variances)

**Question:** Test the null hypothesis that two populations have equal variances ($\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2$) using the following data. Test at the $\mathbf{10\%}$ level of significance ($\alpha = 0.10$).
* **Sample 1:** $n_1 = 16$, Sample Variance $S_1^2 = 30$
* **Sample 2:** $n_2 = 11$, Sample Variance $S_2^2 = 12$
* **Critical F-value (Table Value for $\alpha/2=0.05$):** $F_{\text{Crit}}(df_1=15, df_2=10) \approx 2.85$

#### 1. State the Hypotheses and Significance Level
* **$H_0$:** $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2$ (Variances are equal.)
* **$H_1$:** $\sigma_1^2 \ne \sigma_2^2$ (Variances are not equal - Two-Tailed Test.)
* **Significance Level:** $\alpha = 0.10$. For a two-tailed test using the upper tail convention, we use $\alpha/2 = 0.05$.

#### 2. Determine Degrees of Freedom and Critical Value
Since $S_1^2 = 30$ is larger than $S_2^2 = 12$, we set Sample 1 as the numerator.
* **Numerator $df_1$:** $n_1 - 1 = 16 - 1 = 15$
* **Denominator $df_2$:** $n_2 - 1 = 11 - 1 = 10$
* **Critical Value:** $F_{\text{Critical}}$ at $\alpha/2 = 0.05$ with $df_1=15$ and $df_2=10$ is given as $\mathbf{2.85}$.

#### 3. Calculate the $F$-Statistic
$$F_{\text{Calculated}} = \frac{S_{\text{Larger}}^2}{S_{\text{Smaller}}^2} = \frac{S_1^2}{S_2^2} = \frac{30}{12} = \mathbf{2.50}$$

#### 4. Conclusion
* $F_{\text{Calculated}} = 2.50$
* $F_{\text{Critical}} = 2.85$
* Since $2.50 < 2.85$, the calculated $F$-value falls within the acceptance region.

* **Result:** **Accept $H_0$** (Fail to Reject $H_0$).
* **Interpretation:** There is **no significant difference** between the variances of the two populations at the $10\%$ level of significance.

### Example 2: Right-Tailed F-Test (Claiming Higher Variance)

**Question:** A manufacturing process (Process 1) is supposed to have greater variability than the standard process (Process 2). Test this claim at $\alpha = 0.05$.
* **Process 1:** $n_1 = 21$, Sample Variance $S_1^2 = 45$
* **Process 2:** $n_2 = 16$, Sample Variance $S_2^2 = 15$
* **Critical F-value (Table Value for $\alpha=0.05$):** $F_{\text{Crit}}(df_1=20, df_2=15) \approx 2.33$

#### 1. State the Hypotheses and Significance Level
* **Claim:** Process 1 has greater variability ($\sigma_1^2 > \sigma_2^2$). This must be $H_1$.
* **$H_0$:** $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2$
* **$H_1$:** $\sigma_1^2 > \sigma_2^2$ (Right-Tailed Test.)
* **Significance Level:** $\alpha = 0.05$.

#### 2. Determine Degrees of Freedom and Critical Value
Since $H_1$ specifies $\sigma_1^2 > \sigma_2^2$, $S_1^2$ must be the numerator, even though it is larger (this aligns with the F-distribution convention for this specific one-tailed test).
* **Numerator $df_1$:** $n_1 - 1 = 21 - 1 = 20$
* **Denominator $df_2$:** $n_2 - 1 = 16 - 1 = 15$
* **Critical Value:** $F_{\text{Critical}}$ at $\alpha = 0.05$ with $df_1=20$ and $df_2=15$ is given as $\mathbf{2.33}$.

#### 3. Calculate the $F$-Statistic
$$F_{\text{Calculated}} = \frac{S_1^2}{S_2^2} = \frac{45}{15} = \mathbf{3.00}$$

#### 4. Conclusion
* $F_{\text{Calculated}} = 3.00$
* $F_{\text{Critical}} = 2.33$
* Since $3.00 > 2.33$, the calculated $F$-value falls into the rejection region.

* **Result:** **Reject $H_0$** and **Accept $H_1$**.
* **Interpretation:** There is **significant evidence** to support the claim that the manufacturing process (Process 1) has a greater variability than the standard process (Process 2) at the $5\%$ level.

##  Hypothesis Testing (F-test/Test of Variance Equality) Examples

The F-test for comparing two variances ($\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$) is always a **two-tailed test** when we simply check for **"equality"** (i.e., $H_1: \sigma_1^2 \ne \sigma_2^2$).

**Key Rule:** To simplify the process, we always place the **Larger Sample Variance ($S^2$) in the numerator**. This ensures $F_{\text{Calculated}} \ge 1$. For a two-tailed test with significance level $\alpha$, we find the critical value $F_{\text{Critical}}$ using $\alpha/2$.

### Example 1: Testing Equality of Variance (Given Variances)

**Question:** The two random samples reveal the following data:

| Sample no. | Size ($n$) | Mean | Variance ($S^2$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| I | 16 | 440 | 40 |
| II | 25 | 460 | 42 |

Test the **equality of Population Variance** (Assume $\alpha = 0.05$).

#### 1. State the Data and Hypotheses
* Sample I: $n_1=16$, $S_1^2=40$. $df_1 = 16-1 = 15$.
* Sample II: $n_2=25$, $S_2^2=42$. $df_2 = 25-1 = 24$.
* $S_2^2$ (42) is the larger variance. It goes in the numerator.
* $\alpha = 0.05$. Since it's a two-tailed test, we use $\alpha/2 = 0.025$.

* **Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):** $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2$ (The population variances are equal.)
* **Alternate Hypothesis ($H_1$):** $\sigma_1^2 \ne \sigma_2^2$ (The population variances are not equal.)

#### 2. Determine Degrees of Freedom and Critical Value
Since $S_2^2$ is in the numerator:
* Numerator $df_1 = n_2 - 1 = 24$
* Denominator $df_2 = n_1 - 1 = 15$
* **Critical Value ($F_{\text{Critical}}$):** Look up $F$ for $\alpha = 0.025$, $df_1=24$, and $df_2=15$.
    $$F_{\text{Critical}} \approx \mathbf{2.70}$$

#### 3. Calculate the $F$-Statistic
We place the larger variance in the numerator:
$$F_{\text{Calculated}} = \frac{S_2^2}{S_1^2} = \frac{42}{40} = \mathbf{1.05}$$

#### 4. Conclusion
* $F_{\text{Calculated}} = 1.05$
* $F_{\text{Critical}} = 2.70$
* Since $1.05 < 2.70$, the calculated $F$-value falls within the acceptance region.

* **Result:** **Accept $H_0$** (Fail to Reject $H_0$).
* **Interpretation:** There is **no significant difference** between the variances of the two populations.

### Example 2: Testing Equality of Variance (Calculating $S^2$ from Raw Data)

**Question:** The height of **6 randomly sailors** are **63, 65, 68, 69, 71 and 72**. Those of **9 randomly chosen soldiers** are **61, 62, 65, 66, 69, 70, 71, 72 and 73**. Test the **equality of Population Variance** (Test at **5% LOS**).

#### 1. Calculate Sample Variances ($S^2$)
We use the formula $S^2 = \frac{\sum (x-\bar{x})^2}{n-1}$.

* **Sailors (1):** $n_1=6$. $\sum x_1 = 408$. $\bar{x}_1 = 68$.
    $$\sum (x_1-\bar{x}_1)^2 = (63-68)^2 + \dots + (72-68)^2 = 25+9+0+1+9+16 = 60$$
    $$S_1^2 = \frac{60}{6-1} = \mathbf{12}$$
* **Soldiers (2):** $n_2=9$. $\sum x_2 = 609$. $\bar{x}_2 = 67.67$.
    $$\sum (x_2-\bar{x}_2)^2 \approx 135.56$$ (From previous calculation)
    $$S_2^2 = \frac{135.56}{9-1} = \mathbf{16.945}$$

#### 2. Hypotheses and Critical Value
* $S_2^2$ (16.945) is the larger variance. It goes in the numerator.
* $\alpha = 0.05$. Two-tailed, so $\alpha/2 = 0.025$.

* **$H_0$:** $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2$
* **$H_1$:** $\sigma_1^2 \ne \sigma_2^2$
* **Degrees of Freedom:**
    * Numerator $df_1 = n_2 - 1 = 9 - 1 = 8$
    * Denominator $df_2 = n_1 - 1 = 6 - 1 = 5$
* **Critical Value ($F_{\text{Critical}}$):** Look up $F$ for $\alpha = 0.025$, $df_1=8$, and $df_2=5$.
    $$F_{\text{Critical}} \approx \mathbf{6.09}$$

#### 3. Calculate the $F$-Statistic
$$F_{\text{Calculated}} = \frac{S_2^2}{S_1^2} = \frac{16.945}{12} \approx \mathbf{1.412}$$

#### 4. Conclusion
* $F_{\text{Calculated}} = 1.412$
* $F_{\text{Critical}} = 6.09$
* Since $1.412 < 6.09$, the calculated $F$-value is in the acceptance region.

* **Result:** **Accept $H_0$**.
* **Interpretation:** We conclude that there is **no significant difference** between the variability (variance) of heights for sailors and soldiers.

## Quiz Questions Solutions

### Quiz Question 1: Testing Equality of Variance (Raw Data)

**Question:** Test whether the two populations have the **same variances** (Take $\alpha=0.05$).
* **Sample 1:** $n_1=10$. Data: 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 24, 26, 27.
* **Sample 2:** $n_2=12$. Data: 19, 22, 23, 25, 26, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 35, 36.

#### 1. Calculate Sample Variances ($S^2$)
* **Sample 1:** $\sum x_1 = 210$. $\bar{x}_1 = 210/10 = 21$.
    $$\sum (x_1-\bar{x}_1)^2 = 114$$
    $$S_1^2 = \frac{114}{10-1} = \mathbf{12.67}$$
* **Sample 2:** $\sum x_2 = 336$. $\bar{x}_2 = 336/12 = 28$.
    $$\sum (x_2-\bar{x}_2)^2 = 262$$
    $$S_2^2 = \frac{262}{12-1} = \mathbf{23.82}$$

#### 2. Hypotheses and Critical Value
* $S_2^2$ (23.82) is the larger variance. It goes in the numerator.
* $\alpha = 0.05$. Two-tailed, so $\alpha/2 = 0.025$.

* **$H_0$:** $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2$
* **$H_1$:** $\sigma_1^2 \ne \sigma_2^2$
* **Degrees of Freedom:**
    * Numerator $df_1 = n_2 - 1 = 12 - 1 = 11$
    * Denominator $df_2 = n_1 - 1 = 10 - 1 = 9$
* **Critical Value ($F_{\text{Critical}}$):** Look up $F$ for $\alpha = 0.025$, $df_1=11$, and $df_2=9$.
    $$F_{\text{Critical}} \approx \mathbf{4.39}$$

#### 3. Calculate the $F$-Statistic
$$F_{\text{Calculated}} = \frac{S_2^2}{S_1^2} = \frac{23.82}{12.67} \approx \mathbf{1.88}$$

#### 4. Conclusion
* $F_{\text{Calculated}} = 1.88$
* $F_{\text{Critical}} = 4.39$
* Since $1.88 < 4.39$, the calculated $F$-value is in the acceptance region.

* **Result:** **Accept $H_0$**.
* **Interpretation:** We conclude that the two populations have the **same variances**.

### Quiz Question 2: Testing Difference in Variances (Given Variances, Large Samples)

**Question:** Supplier A: **41 samples**, variance **110 hours**. Supplier B: **21 samples**, variance **65 hours**. At a **0.05 alpha level** determine if there is a **difference in the variances**.

* Supplier A (1): $n_1=41$, $S_1^2=110$. $df_1 = 40$.
* Supplier B (2): $n_2=21$, $S_2^2=65$. $df_2 = 20$.
* $S_1^2$ (110) is the larger variance. It goes in the numerator.
* $\alpha = 0.05$. Two-tailed ("difference"), so $\alpha/2 = 0.025$.

#### 1. Hypotheses and Critical Value
* **$H_0$:** $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2$
* **$H_1$:** $\sigma_1^2 \ne \sigma_2^2$
* **Degrees of Freedom:**
    * Numerator $df_1 = n_1 - 1 = 40$
    * Denominator $df_2 = n_2 - 1 = 20$
* **Critical Value ($F_{\text{Critical}}$):** Look up $F$ for $\alpha = 0.025$, $df_1=40$, and $df_2=20$.
    $$F_{\text{Critical}} \approx \mathbf{2.33}$$

#### 2. Calculate the $F$-Statistic
$$F_{\text{Calculated}} = \frac{S_1^2}{S_2^2} = \frac{110}{65} \approx \mathbf{1.692}$$

#### 3. Conclusion
* $F_{\text{Calculated}} = 1.692$
* $F_{\text{Critical}} = 2.33$
* Since $1.692 < 2.33$, the calculated $F$-value is in the acceptance region.

* **Result:** **Accept $H_0$**.
* **Interpretation:** There is **no statistically significant difference** between the variances of the battery life from Supplier A and Supplier B.
