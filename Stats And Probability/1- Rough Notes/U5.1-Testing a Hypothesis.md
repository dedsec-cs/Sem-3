## Statistics And Probability: Hypothesis Tests and Control Charts

## Test Of Hypothesis

A **Hypothesis Test** is a formal procedure for deciding between two competing ideas (hypotheses) about a **population** based on evidence collected from a **sample**.

Imagine a large factory produces light bulbs (**the population**). A worker claims the average lifespan of these bulbs is 1,000 hours (**the hypothesis**). To check this claim, you take a small batch of bulbs (**the sample**) and measure their average lifespan.

* **The Goal:** We want to know if the information (the claim) about the population characteristic (average lifespan) can be **accepted** or **rejected**.
* **The Method:** We collect data from a small, representative part (the **sample**) and use that data to make a confident conclusion about the entire **population**.

---

## Symbols Of Population and Sample

In statistics, we use different symbols to distinguish between information about the large population and information gathered from the small sample.

| Population Parameter/Characteristic | Sample Statistic | Description |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\text{Population Size} (N)$ | $\text{Sample Size} (n)$ | The total number of items in the population vs. the number of items collected for testing. |
| $\text{Population Mean} (\mu)$ | $\text{Sample Mean} (\bar{x})$ | The true average of the whole population (usually unknown) vs. the average calculated from the sample. |
| $\text{Population Standard Deviation} (\sigma)$ | $\text{Sample Standard Deviation} (S)$ | The spread or variability of the whole population vs. the spread calculated from the sample. |
| $\text{Population Variance} (\sigma^2)$ | $\text{Sample Variance} (S^2)$ | The square of the standard deviation. |

## Procedure Of Test Of Hypothesis

A hypothesis test follows a set of structured steps to ensure the decision is logical and statistically sound.

1.  **State Null Hypothesis ($\mathbf{H_0}$) & Alternate Hypothesis ($\mathbf{H_1}$):** Formulate the two competing claims.
2.  **Define Level of Significance ($\boldsymbol{\alpha}$):** Decide how much risk of being wrong you are willing to accept.
3.  **Use Suitable Test Statistic:** Choose the correct formula (e.g., $z$-test, $t$-test) to crunch the sample numbers.
4.  **Establish Critical Region (Table Value):** Determine the cut-off point for the test statistic, based on the chosen significance level.
5.  **Conclusion:** Compare your calculated result to the cut-off point and make the decision to **Accept** or **Reject** the Null Hypothesis.

### Null Hypothesis ($H_0$)

The **Null Hypothesis** ($H_0$) is the initial assumption or the 'status quo' that we assume to be true. It always states that there is **no significant difference** or **no change** in the population parameter.

* **Assumption:** We start by assuming that the sample result ($\bar{x}$) is just a fluke and the population parameter ($\mu$) is exactly as claimed.
* **Wording:** It always contains an equality sign ($=$, $\le$, or $\ge$).
    * **Example:** $H_0$: The average lifespan of a light bulb is 1,000 hours ($\mu = 1000$).

### Alternative Hypothesis ($H_1$)

The **Alternative Hypothesis** ($H_1$) is the statement we hope to prove. It contradicts the null hypothesis and asserts that a **significant difference** or **change** *does* exist.

* **Wording:** It always contains an inequality sign ($\ne$, $>$, or $<$ ).
    * **Example (Claim of Change):** $H_1$: The average lifespan is **not** 1,000 hours ($\mu \ne 1000$).

#### Single-Tailed vs. Two-Tailed Test

The form of the alternative hypothesis determines whether we use a one-tailed or two-tailed test. Let's assume $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ (where $\mu_0$ is the assumed value).

| Alternate Hypothesis ($H_1$) | Type of Test | Focus of Rejection |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\mu \mathbf{\ne} \mu_0$ | **Two-Tailed Test** | Rejection can happen if $\mu$ is significantly **higher** *or* **lower** than $\mu_0$. |
| $\mu \mathbf{>} \mu_0$ | **Single (Right) Tailed Test** | Rejection only happens if $\mu$ is significantly **higher** than $\mu_0$. |
| $\mu \mathbf{<} \mu_0$ | **Single (Left) Tailed Test** | Rejection only happens if $\mu$ is significantly **lower** than $\mu_0$. |



### Level of Significance ($\alpha$)

The **Level of Significance** ($\alpha$) is the maximum probability of **rejecting the Null Hypothesis ($H_0$) when it is actually true**. This is a risk we accept, and it is set *before* the test begins.

* $\alpha$ is often called the probability of a **Type I Error** (false positive).
* **Common Values:** $\alpha = 0.05$ (or **5%**) and $\alpha = 0.01$ (or **1%**).
    * If $\alpha = 5\%$, you are willing to be wrong 5 out of 100 times when you decide to reject $H_0$.

### Critical Value and Critical Region

#### Critical Region (or Rejection Region)

The **Critical Region** is the area in the distribution (usually a Normal or $t$-distribution) where, if the **calculated test statistic** falls, we must **Reject the Null Hypothesis ($H_0$)**. This area corresponds to the $\alpha$ level.

#### Critical Value

The **Critical Value** is the boundary line (a specific numerical value) that separates the Critical Region (Rejection Region) from the **Acceptance Region**.

The value depends on:
1.  **Level of Significance ($\alpha$):** How large the rejection area is.
2.  **Alternative Hypothesis ($H_1$):** Whether the area is split into two tails or concentrated in one tail (left or right).

$$
\text{If } \alpha = 5\% \text{ (0.05) and we use a Z-test (large sample):}
$$

| Test Type | $H_1$ | Critical Value(s) (Z-Scores) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Two-Tailed** | $\mu \ne \mu_0$ | $\pm 1.96$ (0.025 in each tail) |
| **Single Left-Tailed** | $\mu < \mu_0$ | $-1.645$ (0.05 in the left tail) |
| **Single Right-Tailed** | $\mu > \mu_0$ | $+1.645$ (0.05 in the right tail) |

### Conclusion

The final step is to compare the value of the **Test Statistic (Calculated Value)** to the **Critical Value (Table Value)**.

* **Decision Rule:**
    * **If** $| \text{Calculated Value} | \le | \text{Critical Value} |$: The calculated value falls in the **Acceptance Region**. We **Accept $H_0$** (or **Fail to Reject $H_0$**). The evidence is not strong enough to reject the initial assumption.
    * **If** $| \text{Calculated Value} | > | \text{Critical Value} |$: The calculated value falls in the **Critical/Rejection Region**. We **Reject $H_0$** and **Accept $H_1$**. The evidence strongly suggests the initial assumption is false.
