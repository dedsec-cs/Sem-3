## Test Of Hypothesis For Large Samples And Small Samples

When performing a hypothesis test, the size of the sample ($n$) is crucial because it determines which statistical distribution and formula we use. The general dividing line between a large and a small sample is a sample size of **$n = 30$**.

---

### Difference between Large and Small Sample

| Feature | Large Sample ($n > 30$) | Small Sample ($n \le 30$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Sample Size** | The sample size is **greater than 30** ($n > 30$). | The sample size is **less than or equal to 30** ($n \le 30$). |
| **Population Estimate** | The value of the sample statistic (like the sample standard deviation, $S$) is a very reliable estimate of the corresponding population parameter ($\sigma$). | The sample statistic is **not** a reliable enough estimate of the population parameter. |
| **Test Distribution** | The **Normal distribution** (using the **$Z$-test**) is used for testing, thanks to the **Central Limit Theorem**. | Sampling distributions like the **$t$-distribution**, $F$-distribution, or Chi-square ($\chi^2$) distribution are used for testing. |

> **Analogy:** A large sample ($n > 30$) gives you enough information to assume the sample mean is distributed normally, allowing you to use the standard $Z$-test. A small sample ($n \le 30$) is riskier and requires a more conservative approach using the **$t$-distribution**, which accounts for this greater uncertainty.

---

### Test Of Hypothesis For Large Samples ($n > 30$)

For large samples, we primarily use the **$Z$-test**. This approach is justified by the **Central Limit Theorem**, which states that the distribution of sample means tends to be normal, regardless of the population's original distribution, as long as the sample size is large.

Two important applications include:

1.  **Test of hypothesis about a single population mean** ($\mu$).
2.  **Test of hypothesis about the difference between two population means** ($\mu_1 - \mu_2$).

#### Test Of Hypothesis About Population Mean

This test is used when we want to check if the true average ($\mu$) of a population is equal to a specific claimed value ($\mu_0$).

**1. Formulating Hypotheses:**

* **Null Hypothesis** ($H_0$): $\mu = \mu_0$ (The population mean is the specified value).
* **Alternate Hypothesis** ($H_1$): $\mu \ne \mu_0$ (Two-tailed) OR $\mu > \mu_0$ (Right-tailed) OR $\mu < \mu_0$ (Left-tailed).

**2. Test Statistic ($Z$):**

The formula used to calculate the test statistic, $Z$, tells us how many standard errors the sample mean ($\bar{x}$) is away from the hypothesized population mean ($\mu$):

$$Z =\frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}}$$

Where:
* $\bar{x}$ = Sample Mean
* $\mu$ = Hypothesized Population Mean (from $H_0$)
* $n$ = Sample Size
* $\sigma$ = Population Standard Deviation

**Important Note for Large Samples:** If the true population standard deviation ($\sigma$) is unknown (which is common), we can confidently substitute it with the **Sample Standard Deviation ($S$)** because $n > 30$. That is, $\sigma \approx S$.

**3. Critical Values ($Z_{\text{Critical}}$):**

The critical value is found using the standard Normal Distribution table based on the chosen level of significance ($\alpha$) and the type of test (one or two-tailed).

| Level of Significance ($\alpha$) | Two-Tailed Test ($H_1: \mu \ne \mu_0$) | One-Tailed Test ($H_1: \mu > \mu_0$ or $\mu < \mu_0$) |
| :------------------------------- | :------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------- |
| **1%** ($\alpha=0.01$)           | $\mathbf{Z= 2.58}$                     | $\mathbf{Z= 2.33}$                                    |
| **5%** ($\alpha=0.05$)           | $\mathbf{Z = 1.96}$                    | $\mathbf{Z= 1.645}$                                   |

**4. Conclusion:**

* Calculate the value of $Z$ using the formula.
* Compare the absolute calculated $Z$-value ($|Z|$) with the appropriate critical $Z$-value from the table.
* If $|Z_{\text{Calculated}}| \le |Z_{\text{Critical}}|$, **Accept $H_0$**.
* If $|Z_{\text{Calculated}}| > |Z_{\text{Critical}}|$, **Reject $H_0$**.
